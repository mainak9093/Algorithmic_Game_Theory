\subsection*{2026-08-01 --- What the two negative results jointly require}

Taking Theorem~\ref{thm:obstruction} and Proposition~\ref{prop:msw-false}
together, any correct algorithm must be free to (i) move already-allocated
chores between bundles and (ii) give up total cost while doing so. Every
construction that currently works does so by exhibiting a per-agent potential
$\phi$ with $\edgew{\alloc}(i,j) \le \phi_i - \phi_j$, which telescopes along
paths and vanishes around cycles. \emph{Finding such a potential for general
negative dichotomous costs, or proving that none exists, is the problem.} In
Theorem~\ref{thm:binadd} the potential is $\phi_i = u_i$, the count of
universally disliked chores held; in Theorem~\ref{thm:identical} it is
$\phi_i = \cost(\bundle{i})$. What is the right $\phi$ in general?

Worth noting that the existence of such a $\phi$ is not merely sufficient but
close to necessary: if $\optsubsidy \in \set{0,1}^n$ then $\phi_i :=
\optsubsidy_i$ satisfies $\edgew{\alloc}(i,j) \le \phi_i - \phi_j$ by definition
of an envy-free solution. So the search for a potential and the search for a
proof are the same search, and the content is in constructing $\phi$ and the
allocation \emph{together}.

\subsection*{2026-08-03 --- routes surviving the two no-go results}

\Cref{sec:approach4} closes valuation-level encoding and algorithm-level weight
forcing, and Corollary~\ref{cor:no-refinement} closes every rule that selects
inside the utilitarian-optimal set. What remains splits by which of those
failures it avoids.

\textbf{Route M --- iterated matching.} \cite[\S3]{LMS26} develops the
objective-chores analogue of iterated maximum-weight \emph{perfect} matching,
padding with zero-valued dummies so every agent takes one object per round,
which makes the output balanced. The obvious lift to our setting --- each round
assign every agent one remaining chore minimising the total marginal
$\sum_i [\cost_i(\bundle{i} \cup \set{g_i}) - \cost_i(\bundle{i})]$ --- does
\emph{not} give $\pathw{} \le 1$ for non-additive dichotomous costs: it fails on
$13/250$ instances at $n=3,m=4$ and $10/120$ at $n=3,m=5$ (\texttt{rules.py}).
That is diagnostic rather than discouraging, and \cite{LMS26} flags the obstacle
itself: the telescoping needs additivity, because an additive round-$t$ cost is
independent of what came before whereas a dichotomous marginal depends on the
bundle already held. The repair to attempt is a round invariant replacing the
additive domination --- each round's matching minimum-cost \emph{conditioned on
the current bundles}, with marginals non-increasing across rounds, a ``peel the
free chores first'' discipline. \textbf{Status:} the naive lift is refuted; the
conditioned version is untested.

\textbf{Route A --- induct on agents, not items.} Every approach so far inducts
on items. \cite[\S4.3.1]{LMS26} instead maintains a set of active agents, adds
one at a time, and restores two invariants: compensated utility at least
$\lambda$ with $\lambda \le \subsidy_i \le 1$, and an equality path from every
active agent to the set of minimum compensated utility. For chores this is
attractive because a newcomer starts with an empty bundle, which is the
\emph{cheapest} bundle, so the newcomer is the natural sink and the orientation
problem of \Cref{sec:approach1} does not arise. The invariant carried is the
equality graph rather than a subsidy scalar --- which is precisely the extra
information Theorem~\ref{thm:f2} and Proposition~\ref{prop:f4} say a recipient
rule needs. \textbf{Status:} untried, and the most promising of these.

\textbf{Route N --- non-redundancy instead of coverage.} Theorem~\ref{thm:f2}
says the failing placements are exactly the zero-marginal ones, and the
literature already forbids those under the name non-wastefulness. A duplicate is
redundant for its holder by construction, so ask for a \emph{non-redundant}
allocation of the replica set --- which General Yankee Swap is built to maintain
--- and measure how far non-redundancy falls short of coverage. The two are not
equivalent: a duplicate can be redundant for everyone when every other agent
already holds the type or has zero marginal for it. But the residual gap is
narrower than coverage itself and is exactly characterisable. \textbf{Status:}
cheap to test, untested.

\textbf{Route X --- matroid exchange.} As in \Cref{sec:mrf}. Highest ceiling,
highest cost; the parallel-extension step needs a full proof before anything is
built on it.

\textbf{Ranking.} Route A, then Route M's conditioned repair, then Route N,
then Route X. Conjecture~\ref{conj:h1pp} --- the type-ordered peel --- stays
live alongside them and has the best evidence of anything currently open.

\subsection*{2026-08-06 --- a cleaner rule than construct-and-repair}

\textbf{The idea.} \emph{Among cardinality-balanced allocations of minimum total
cost, take one minimising the number of envious ordered pairs
$\#\set{(i,j) : \cost_i(\bundle{i}) > \cost_i(\bundle{j})}$.} A single two-level
optimisation: no local search, no restarts, no schedule, no warm start.

\textbf{Why it might work.} It sidesteps Corollary~\ref{cor:no-refinement}
because the counterexample there has a \emph{unique} optimum of sizes
$(0,3,1)$, which is not balanced --- restricting the optimisation to balanced
allocations removes it from the feasible set entirely. A cost-minimising
balanced allocation is automatically envy-freeable, since any reassignment of
its own bundles is another balanced allocation and so cannot be cheaper, which
means only $\pathw{} \le 1$ remains to be shown. The second criterion is the one
aligned with Proposition~\ref{prop:twotier}: in a $\set{0,1}$ solution envy runs
only from the paid tier to the unpaid one, so minimising envy count pushes
towards exactly the two-tier shape.

\textbf{Status.} Zero failures in the \emph{strong} form --- every allocation
the rule can select is good --- over $41{,}196$ instances: exhaustive over all
dichotomous triples at $n = m = 3$, exhaustive over structured families at
$m \le 4$, and endpoint-constant sweeps to $n = 5$, $m = 6$
(\texttt{update\_8/ruleD\_adv.py}). Without the tie-break the cost-minimising
balanced set always \emph{contains} a good allocation but is not uniformly good;
the three other natural tie-breaks --- leximin own-cost, minimum total perceived
load, leximin perceived load --- each fail at $n = 3$, $m = 4$
(\texttt{tiebreak\_balanced.py}).

\textbf{What would kill it.} A balanced instance where every minimum-cost,
minimum-envy allocation needs subsidy $2$. \textbf{Not proved}, at any $n$: an
attempt at $n = 3$ stalled on the exchange step, because dichotomous costs need
not be submodular and a bundle of positive cost need not contain any single item
whose removal lowers it --- so ``$\cost_u(\bundle{u})$ is large, therefore some
swap improves'' is unavailable. That is the same missing exchange property that
makes the matroid-rank subclass (\Cref{sec:mrf}) the natural next target: there
it \emph{is} available.

\subsection*{2026-08-06 --- at most two distinct cost functions}

\textbf{The idea.} Suppose the $n$ agents carry only \emph{two} distinct cost
functions $\cost$ and $\cost'$, in groups of sizes $k$ and $n-k$. This strictly
generalises the identical-cost case of Theorem~\ref{thm:identical} and is not
covered by anything cited here: the literature settles identical, additive and
submodular costs, but ``two types of agents'' is a different slice --- one that
does appear in the house-allocation work of~\cite{DCWXZ24} as a tractable
restriction, which is mild evidence it is the right kind of hypothesis.

\textbf{Why it might work.} The two-tier conditions collapse dramatically. For
two agents of the same type in the same tier, (a) forces
$\cost(\bundle{i}) = \cost(\bundle{j})$ exactly; for two of the same type in
different tiers, (b) and (c) force
$\cost(\bundle{i}) = \cost(\bundle{j}) + 1$ exactly. So within each type group
the bundle costs take at most two values, differing by exactly one, and the
tier split inside that group is determined by which value a bundle takes. What
remains is a finite system of cross-group inequalities in two functions rather
than $n$, which is the kind of thing a direct construction can attack.

\textbf{Status.} Untried as a proof. Zero failures over $7{,}805$ two-type
instances at $m \in \set{3,4}$, $n \in \set{3,4}$, both for existence and for
the rule of the previous entry (\texttt{update\_9/structural.py}). Worth one
session: it is the first class in a while where the conditions genuinely
simplify rather than merely being restricted.

\textbf{What would kill it.} A two-type instance with no good allocation. Note
this would also refute Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} outright, so the search is
worth running for its own sake.

\subsection*{2026-08-06 --- induct on the depth of the cost functions}

\textbf{The idea.} Stop inducting on items, on agents, or on schedules, and
induct on the \emph{cost functions themselves}. Every dichotomous $\cost$
decomposes into nested monotone Boolean layers: putting $\mathcal{F}_t :=
\set{S : \cost(S) \ge t}$, each $\mathcal{F}_t$ is upward-closed,
$\mathcal{F}_1 \supseteq \dots \supseteq \mathcal{F}_K$ with $K = \cost(\items)$,
and
\[
  \cost(S) \;=\; \#\set{t : S \in \mathcal{F}_t}
  \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{K} \mathbb{1}\big[S \in \mathcal{F}_t\big].
\]
Crucially the class is closed under truncation: $\min(\cost, k)$ is dichotomous
for every $k$, so layers can be peeled one at a time without leaving the model.
Define the \emph{depth} of an instance to be $K = \max_i \cost_i(\items)$.

\textbf{Why it might work.} The base case is already proved. A depth-one
instance has every $\cost_i$ taking values in $\set{0,1}$, so every bundle costs
at most $1$ to everybody --- which is exactly the hypothesis of
Theorem~\ref{thm:smallbundle}. So \emph{Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} holds
unconditionally at depth $1$, for every $n$ and every $m$}. No other induction
in this project has a proved base case, and none of the failed approaches
touches the representation of the cost functions at all: they all manipulate
allocations while treating $\cost_i$ as a black-box oracle.

\textbf{Evidence for the inductive step.} Over all $1{,}330$ depth-$\ge 2$
instances at $n = m = 3$, the full instance and its truncation
$(\min(\cost_i, K-1))_i$ \emph{always} share at least one common good
allocation --- $100\%$, with no instance where one is solvable and the other is
not (\texttt{update\_10/layers.py}). That is precisely the compatibility an
induction on depth needs.

\textbf{The kill condition, hunted and not found.} A depth-$\ge 2$ instance
whose good allocations are disjoint from its truncation's would end the
approach. Across $39{,}692$ depth-$\ge 2$ instances --- exhaustive over all
dichotomous triples at $n = m = 3$, exhaustive over structured families at
$m \le 4$, and endpoint-constant and deliberately high-depth sweeps to $n = 5$
--- there is \textbf{no such instance}, in either peeling direction
(\texttt{update\_10/layer\_hunt.py}). This is the generator that refuted
Proposition~\ref{prop:no-balance} on its first run.

The same sweep says which direction to peel. Measuring the fraction of the
truncation's good allocations that survive into the full instance, top-peeling
$\cost \mapsto \min(\cost, K-1)$ carries far more than bottom-peeling
$\cost \mapsto \max(\cost - 1, 0)$ --- worst case $0.21$ against $0.05$ --- and
on high-depth instances top-peeling carries \emph{all} of them, at
$n \in \set{3,4,5}$.

\textbf{The strengthened hypothesis, and why it is the right one.} Sharing a
witness is necessary but not sufficient: an induction must \emph{name} which
good allocation to carry forward. The fix is induction loading --- prove
something stronger, so that the inductive hypothesis is strong enough to
reproduce itself.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Chain conjecture.} For every dichotomous instance of depth $K$ there is
a \emph{single} allocation that is good for $(\min(\cost_i, k))_{i}$
\emph{simultaneously} for every $k = 1, \dots, K$.
\end{quote}

Its top level $k = K$ is Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}; its bottom level $k = 1$ is
Theorem~\ref{thm:smallbundle}, already proved. It has \textbf{zero failures over
$30{,}405$ depth-$\ge 2$ instances} across the same exhaustive and adversarial
sweeps. Being stronger it is a far better induction hypothesis than
Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} itself, since carrying a whole chain of certificates
upward is exactly what the step needs and what the plain statement cannot
supply.

\textbf{Progress on the step: the chain collapses to two levels.} Write
$a_{ij} := \cost_i(\bundle{j})$ for the cost matrix of an allocation. Every
level-$k$ arc depends on the matrix alone,
\[
  \edgew{}^{(k)}(i,j) \;=\; \min(a_{ii},k) - \min(a_{ij},k),
\]
and is therefore \emph{sign-constant and saturating}: it has the sign of
$\edgew{}(i,j)$ throughout, its modulus is non-decreasing in $k$, and it reaches
$\abs{\edgew{}(i,j)}$ once $k \ge \max(a_{ii},a_{ij})$. One consequence is
immediate and proved.

\begin{lemma}[Level one is exactly envy-freeability]
\label{lem:levelone}
An allocation is good for $(\min(\cost_i,1))_i$ if and only if it is
envy-freeable for $(\min(\cost_i,1))_i$. No subsidy condition is needed.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\min(\cost_i,1)$ takes values in $\set{0,1}$, so every bundle costs at most $1$
to every agent and Theorem~\ref{thm:smallbundle} applies to any envy-freeable
allocation. The converse holds because goodness includes envy-freeability.
\end{proof}

\textbf{A two-level reduction was proposed here and is now REFUTED.} It rested
on two statements about a single allocation's set of good levels:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{(I) Interval.} That set is always an interval.\\
\textbf{(II) Endpoints.} Good at level $1$ and level $K$ implies good at every
level between.
\end{quote}
Both survived $2{,}288{,}509$ (instance, allocation) pairs, and both are
\textbf{false}.

The refutation came from a change of search space that is worth keeping. Since
the level-$k$ arcs depend only on the matrix $a$, and \emph{every} non-negative
integer matrix is realisable --- take $\cost_i(S) := \sum_j \min(\abs{S \cap
\bundle{j}}, a_{ij})$, whose marginals are all $0$ or $1$, with bundles of size
$\max_i a_{ij}$ --- claims (I) and (II) are equivalent to statements about all
integer matrices, which can be \emph{enumerated} rather than sampled. Doing so
finds $972$ counterexamples among the $261{,}632$ matrices with entries at most
$3$ (\texttt{update\_11/matrix\_interval.py}). The smallest is
\[
  a \;=\; \big(\cost_i(\bundle{j})\big) \;=\;
  \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&0 \\ 2&2&1 \\ 3&3&2 \end{pmatrix},
\]
realised at $n = 3$, $m = 8$ by $\cost_1 \equiv 0$ and the two formulas above.
That allocation is good at level $1$, has a \emph{positive cycle} at level $2$,
and is good again at levels $3$ through $8$ --- so its good-level set
$\set{1,3,4,\dots,8}$ is not an interval, and (II) fails at its endpoints
(\texttt{update\_11/verify\_cex.py}). Every earlier sweep missed it only because
none reached $m = 8$.

\textbf{What survives.} The chain conjecture is untouched: (I) and (II) concern
one allocation's level set, whereas the chain conjecture asserts the
\emph{existence} of some allocation good at every level. On the refuting
instance a chain witness does exist --- give everything to agent~1, who finds
all of it free. So the collapse to two levels is gone, but the target is not.

\textbf{The chain conjecture survives past the frontier.} Every earlier chain
test stopped at $m = 6$, while the counterexample above lives at $m = 8$, so the
conjecture had never been tried where the level structure is known to
misbehave. Re-run at $m = 7, 8, 9$ for $n = 3$ and at $m \le 8$ for
$n \in \set{4,5}$ --- using both endpoint-constant sampling and the
\emph{matrix-realising} family $\cost_i(S) = \sum_j \min(\abs{S \cap B_j},
a_{ij})$ that produced the refutation --- it has \textbf{zero failures}
(\texttt{update\_12/chain\_hunt.py}). Neither did any instance fail
Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}. So the conjecture held exactly where its own
shortcut broke.

\textbf{A proved reduction.} The natural sufficient condition turns out to be
the strongest possible one, and it is two lines.

\begin{lemma}[Exact envy-freeness implies chain-goodness]
\label{lem:ef-chain}
If $\alloc$ is exactly envy-free for $(\cost_i)_i$ --- that is
$\cost_i(\bundle{i}) \le \cost_i(\bundle{j})$ for all $i,j$ --- then $\alloc$ is
good at \emph{every} level, with zero subsidy at every level.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Exact envy-freeness says $a_{ii} \le a_{ij}$ for all $i,j$. Since
$x \mapsto \min(x,k)$ is non-decreasing, $\min(a_{ii},k) \le \min(a_{ij},k)$,
so every level-$k$ arc $\edgew{}^{(k)}(i,j)$ is at most $0$, for every $k$.
Every cycle is then a sum of non-positive terms, so no positive cycle exists at
any level; and every directed path likewise has weight at most $0$, so
$\pathw{}^{(k)} \equiv 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:chain-residual}
The chain conjecture holds for every instance that admits an exactly envy-free
allocation. Equivalently, the chain conjecture is equivalent to its own
restriction to instances admitting \emph{no} exactly envy-free allocation.
\end{corollary}

This localises the difficulty sharply. By Remark~\ref{rem:exactEF} an exactly
envy-free allocation exists in about $98.6\%$ of adversarial instances, so
Corollary~\ref{cor:chain-residual} disposes of almost everything and leaves a
residual of roughly $1.4\%$. That residual is not a rounding error: it is
precisely the class on which deciding envy-freeness is NP-complete even for
binary additive costs~\cite{BSV21}. Across the $24$ envy-free-free instances
encountered in the sweeps above, every one still had a chain witness, so the
residual is not obviously where a counterexample lives either.

\textbf{Status.} Proved: the decomposition, truncation closure, the base case
(Theorem~\ref{thm:smallbundle}), Lemma~\ref{lem:levelone},
Lemma~\ref{lem:ef-chain} and Corollary~\ref{cor:chain-residual}. Refuted: (I),
(II) and the two-level reduction. Open: the chain conjecture restricted to
instances with no exactly envy-free allocation --- a strictly smaller target
than before, and one that now coincides with the known hardness core rather
than straddling it.

\textbf{The residual class, generated deliberately.} Since EF-free instances are
about $1.4\%$ of the population they must be constructed rather than sampled.
Two sources were used: rejection sampling from the endpoint-constant and
matrix-realising families, and R10's Set-Splitting reduction, which is EF-free
\emph{by construction} on a hypergraph with no proper $2$-colouring ---
the triangle $U = \set{v_1,v_2,v_3}$, $F = \set{v_1v_2, v_1v_3, v_2v_3}$, giving
$n = 5$, $m = 6$ with binary additive costs. Across $785$ EF-free instances so
obtained, \textbf{every one has a chain witness}
(\texttt{update\_13/residual.py}). On the Set-Splitting instance itself there
are $1620$ of them.

Two hypotheses this suggested, and their fates. On Set-Splitting the chain-good
and top-good sets coincide exactly, both $1620$, which raised the possibility
that on the residual the chain conjecture collapses to
Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}. It does not: over $363$ further EF-free instances,
$78$ have strictly fewer chain-good than top-good allocations. So the chain
conjecture remains strictly stronger than Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} even after
Corollary~\ref{cor:chain-residual} removes the easy instances --- there is no
collapse, and the layer structure retains content exactly where the difficulty
lives. Separately, the min-cost-balanced/min-envy rule of the earlier entry
selects a chain witness on $420$ of $422$ residual instances but not all, so it
is not the construction either.

\textbf{The Set-Splitting family, and a correction.} R10's reduction is the only
generator of \emph{certified} EF-free instances, so it was rebuilt as a family
over hypergraphs rather than the single triangle instance of
\texttt{update\_3}. The reconstruction taken from that addendum's summary is
\emph{wrong} and failed its own correctness check --- it produced an EF-free
instance from a splittable hypergraph. Reading~\cite[proof of Theorem~2]{BSV21}
directly supplies the missing clause: when $r < r'$ the padding agents
$e_{r+1},\dots,e_{r'}$ are ``imaginary hyperedges adjacent to the \emph{entire}
set of vertices'', so they cost $1$ on \emph{every} vertex chore, not $0$. With
that fixed the reduction verifies in both directions --- an exactly envy-free
allocation exists precisely on splittable hypergraphs --- across seven
hypergraphs including two YES controls
(\texttt{update\_14/setsplit\_family.py}).

On the corrected NO instances the structure is sharp. Chain-good and top-good
coincide \emph{exactly} on every one of them ($6$, $108$, $72$, $1620$, $1620$,
$3840$ witnesses respectively), and the total subsidy is the same for every
witness of a given instance, always $n-2$. The dominant form is describable: the
$r'$ dummy chores are spread one per agent over $r'$ of the $n = r'+2$ agents,
the two dummy-free agents hold only chores they value at $0$, and \emph{the paid
set is exactly the set of dummy holders}. That account was exact up to $n = 5$ but covered only $96\%$ of the $30{,}276$
witnesses at $n = 6$, $m = 8$. The exceptions are fully explained, and the
explanation removes them.

\textbf{The exceptions are an artefact of padding.} The reduction sets
$r' = \max(q,r)$ and, when $r < r'$, fills the gap with imaginary hyperedges
adjacent to every vertex. Those padding agents do not behave like real edge
agents, which are free on vertex chores outside their own edge. Comparing two
instances at the same $n = 6$ settles it: with $q = 4$, $r = 2$ --- two padding
agents --- the form covers $96.3\%$ and total subsidies of $1$ and $2$ appear
alongside $n-2 = 4$; with $q = 3$, $r = 4$ --- \emph{no} padding --- the form
covers \textbf{$100\%$ of all $28{,}800$ witnesses}, the paid set is the
dummy-holder set in every one, and the total subsidy is $n-2$ with no
exceptions. Every zero-padding instance tested behaves this way.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Characterisation (exact on all zero-padding instances tested).} Let
$(U,F)$ be a NO instance of Set Splitting with $r \ge q$, so that $r' = r$ and
every edge agent is real. In R10's reduced instance an allocation is a chain
witness \emph{if and only if} the $r$ dummy chores are distributed one per agent
among $r$ of the $n = r+2$ agents and the two dummy-free agents hold only chores
they value at $0$. Its paid set is exactly the set of dummy-holders and its
total subsidy is exactly $n - 2 = r$.
\end{quote}

The restriction $r \ge q$ costs nothing: duplicating hyperedges raises $r$
without changing splittability, so Set Splitting stays NP-hard on it and the
family stays certified-hard. This is now an exact description of every chain
witness on an infinite family of hard instances, stated in terms of the
hypergraph rather than of the allocation search --- which is what a proof needs
as its starting point.

\textbf{The non-additive residual, which is the class that actually matters.}
R10's family is binary \emph{additive}, so Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} already
holds on all of it via~\cite{LMS26}, and the characterisation above is the
binary-additive theorem's shape reappearing. The class that carries the open
problem is the \emph{non-additive} envy-free-free instances, which that
reduction cannot produce. Generating $553$ of them by rejection sampling with an
explicit additivity filter settles three things
(\texttt{update\_15/nonadditive\_residual.py}).

\emph{The chain conjecture survives.} All $553$ have a chain witness ---
$79{,}206$ of them in total, none missing. This is the most adversarial test the
open statement has had, being simultaneously envy-free-free and non-additive.

\emph{The Set-Splitting characterisation does not survive.} ``Paid set $=$
holders of universally costly chores'' holds on $22$ of $553$, and ``total
subsidy constant across the witnesses of an instance'' on $56$ of $553$. Both
were exact on the additive family. So they are artefacts of additivity, and a
proof of the characterisation on R10's family would not have generalised --- the
check was worth running before the proof attempt rather than after.

\emph{And it corrects an earlier lead.} The containment $\text{paid} \subseteq
\argmax_i \cost_i(\bundle{i})$, recorded as $164/164$ in
Remark~\ref{rem:paid-empirics}, fails on $141$ of the $553$. That sample had not
been filtered for additivity. The remark is amended accordingly; what survives
there is Proposition~\ref{prop:paid-characterisation}, which is proved rather
than observed.

\textbf{Back at the induction: what set is it aiming at?} Since level-$k$
goodness depends only on $a$, and every non-negative integer matrix is
realisable, ``which matrices are chain-good'' is decidable by enumeration.
Doing it over all $261{,}632$ matrices with entries at most $3$ at $n = 3$
(\texttt{update\_16/chain\_matrices.py}) kills the two natural invariants and
leaves one lemma.

\emph{Refuted: carry a bipartition.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:twotier},
goodness at level $k$ means \emph{some} bipartition $S_k$ satisfies the two-tier
inequalities at that level. If one $S$ worked at every level the induction could
simply carry it. It cannot: $4{,}911$ matrices are chain-good with no single
bipartition valid throughout. \emph{Refuted: the tier set is stable.} The
canonical set $S_k = \set{i : \pathw{}^{(k)}(i) = 1}$ genuinely moves with $k$
--- $12{,}822$ violations. The smallest witness is
\[
  a = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&0 \\ 2&1&2 \\ 2&1&2 \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \pathw{}^{(1)} = (0,0,0), \quad \pathw{}^{(2)} = (1,0,1),
\]
which is chain-good while its paid set grows from $\emptyset$ to
$\set{1,3}$. So no invariant of the form ``fix who gets paid'' can work.

\emph{What survives, and it is provable.}

\begin{lemma}[Breakpoints]
\label{lem:breakpoints}
Let $T := \set{k \in [1,K] : k = a_{ij} \text{ for some } i,j} \cup \set{K}$.
An allocation is good at every level in $[1,K]$ if and only if it is good at
every level in $T$. Hence the chain condition amounts to at most $n^2+1$
tests, \emph{independently of the depth $K$}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Between two consecutive elements of $T$ each $\min(a_{ij},k)$ is affine in $k$
--- equal to the constant $a_{ij}$ if $a_{ij}$ lies below the interval, and to
$k$ if it lies above --- so every arc, and hence every cycle weight, is affine
there. An affine function that is at most $0$ at both endpoints is at most $0$
throughout, so no positive cycle can appear strictly inside an interval.
Given that, $\pathw{}^{(k)}$ is the maximum of the finitely many affine
functions given by simple paths, together with $0$; a maximum of affine
functions is convex, and a convex function at most $1$ at both endpoints is at
most $1$ throughout.
\end{proof}

Verified with $0$ violations over the same $261{,}632$ matrices. The
consequence is worth stating: the chain conjecture's difficulty does
\emph{not} grow with depth. Layers were introduced to give the induction a
parameter to climb, and Lemma~\ref{lem:breakpoints} says the climb is bounded by
$n^2+1$ steps no matter how tall the stack. An induction on $K$ is therefore the
wrong shape --- the right parameter is the number of distinct cost values an
allocation exhibits, which is at most $n^2$ and has nothing to do with $K$.

\textbf{Method note, worth more than any single result here.} For any question
about a \emph{fixed} allocation, work with the cost matrix and enumerate rather
than sampling instances: every non-negative integer matrix is realisable, so
matrix enumeration is exhaustive over a space that instance enumeration only
samples, and it reaches effective sizes instance search cannot. The
(I)/(II) counterexample sat two items beyond every sweep that preceded it and
fell out of matrix enumeration immediately.

\subsection*{2026-08-06 --- how big must the paid set be, and what the
target really is}

\textbf{Two corrections first.} (i) The previous entry closed by suggesting the
$n=3$ analysis was now small enough to do by hand, on the grounds that
Corollary~\ref{cor:n3-six} makes the $2$-path $i \to j \to k$ free whenever
$a_{ki} \le a_{kk}+1$. Counting how many \emph{hard pairs} --- ordered pairs with
$a_{ki} > a_{kk}+1$ --- a good matrix can carry gives up to $6$ at $n = 3$
(\texttt{update\_17}), so a case analysis branched on the hard-pair pattern has
$2^6$ cases, not a handful. That framing was oversold. (ii) The first
measurement run minimised the total subsidy over \emph{all} allocations, which is
the wrong quantity: it returns spikes such as $\subsidy = (2,0,0)$, cheap in
total but outside $\set{0,1}^n$ and so irrelevant to
Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}. The corrected script
\texttt{update\_17/minimal\_S.py} minimises $\abs{S}$ over \emph{good}
allocations.

\textbf{What the measurement says.} Over $11{,}163$ instances at $n \in
\set{3,4,5}$, $m \le 6$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
minimal $\abs{S}$ over good allocations & instances \\
\midrule
$0$ (exactly envy-free)      & $10{,}559$ \quad($94.6\%$) \\
$1$                          & $486$ \quad($4.4\%$) \\
$2$                          & $93$ \quad($0.8\%$) \\
$3$                          & $24$ \quad($0.2\%$) \\
$4$                          & $1$ \\
\midrule
no good allocation at all    & $0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The maximum of the minimal $\abs{S}$ is exactly $n-1$ at every $n$ tested, always
with witness $\subsidy = (1,\dots,1,0)$. So the answer to ``is the truth stronger
than Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}?'' is \emph{no}: no constant bound, and no bound
growing more slowly than $n-1$, can hold. Extracting the smallest extremal
instance returns $n$ identical additive agents with $n-1$ unit chores --- which is
Example~\ref{ex:lowerbound}, already in the report. The run therefore
re-derives the known lower bound rather than finding a new one; what it does add
is exhaustive confirmation of that example's ``no allocation does better''
clause, for $n \le 7$ (\texttt{update\_17/tight\_family.py}).

\textbf{Two things that are new.} First,
Proposition~\ref{prop:zero-coordinate}: $\optsubsidy$ always has a zero
coordinate, so the total-subsidy clause of Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} follows
from the per-agent one and the target is the single inequality $\max_i
\pathw{\alloc}(i) \le 1$. Second, in $9$ of the instances the minimum total over
all allocations is strictly below the minimum over good ones --- at $n=4$, $2$
against $3$. So Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} is \emph{not} the statement that the
utilitarian-optimal subsidy is at most $n-1$; the $\set{0,1}$ shape genuinely
costs money, and any argument that reasons about the total alone will prove the
wrong theorem.

\textbf{The reframing worth carrying forward.} Reading the two-tier
characterisation as a statement about matrices removes the subsidy vector
entirely:
\[
  \text{Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}}
  \iff
  \exists\, \alloc,\ \exists\, S \subseteq \agents \ :\
  \cost_i(\bundle{j}) \;\ge\; \cost_i(\bundle{i})
    - [\,i \in S\,] + [\,j \in S\,]
  \quad \forall\, i \ne j .
\]
What is left is a partition and a \emph{bipartition} --- and there are only $2^n$
of the latter. This is the first form of the problem in which the existential
over subsidies is finite and small: guess $S$, then ask whether a partition
meeting a fixed system of local constraints exists. Every earlier formulation
had to discover the subsidies and the bundles simultaneously.

\textbf{Status.} Proposition~\ref{prop:zero-coordinate} proved. Tightness
confirmed for $n \le 7$. Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} unrefuted over these
$11{,}163$ instances. Next: for fixed $S$, characterise when the constraint
system is satisfiable --- note that $S = \agents$ and $S = \emptyset$ impose
\emph{the same} constraints, namely exact envy-freeness, which is why the paid
set is never everybody.
