\section{Approach 2: Selecting a Utilitarian-Optimal Allocation}
\label{sec:approach2}

Theorem~\ref{thm:obstruction} rules out building the allocation one chore at a
time. The natural response is to stop building it at all and instead
\emph{select} a good allocation globally, from a set small enough to reason
about and rich enough to contain a witness. Utilitarian optimality is the
obvious candidate for two reasons. First, by
Theorem~\ref{thm:hs-characterisation} an allocation minimising $\sum_i
\cost_i(\bundle{i})$ over \emph{all} allocations, and not merely over
reassignments of its own bundles, is automatically envy-freeable, so half of
what we need is free. Second, it carries a strong exchange property: if
$g \in \bundle{i}$ is \emph{active} for $i$, meaning
$\cost_i(\bundle{i}) - \cost_i(\bundle{i} \setminus \set{g}) = 1$, then
$\cost_j(\bundle{j} \cup \set{g}) = \cost_j(\bundle{j}) + 1$ for every $j$ ---
nobody can absorb it for free, or the allocation would not have been optimal.
Combined with Theorem~\ref{thm:cyclebound}, a path of weight at least $2$ from
$u$ to $v$ forces $\cost_v(\bundle{u}) \ge \cost_v(\bundle{v}) + 2$, so
$\bundle{u}$ carries at least two units that are live for $v$, and one would
like to transfer one of them along the path and show a potential drops.

This is what Conjecture~\ref{conj:msw} proposes. It is false.

% --------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Utilitarian optimality is the wrong restriction}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:msw-false}
Conjecture~\ref{conj:msw} is false. There is an instance with $n = 3$ agents and
$m = 4$ chores whose \emph{unique} utilitarian-optimal allocation requires a
subsidy of $2$, although another allocation --- of strictly greater total cost
--- admits a subsidy of $0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take $\items = \set{g_1,g_2,g_3,g_4}$ and the costs tabulated below.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{\hspace{2.5em}}lccc@{}}
\toprule
$S$ & $\cost_1$ & $\cost_2$ & $\cost_3$ &
$S$ & $\cost_1$ & $\cost_2$ & $\cost_3$ \\
\midrule
$\emptyset$      & 0 & 0 & 0 & $g_3g_4$       & 2 & 1 & 1 \\
$g_1$            & 1 & 1 & 1 & $g_1g_2g_3$    & 2 & 3 & 2 \\
$g_2$            & 1 & 1 & 1 & $g_1g_2g_4$    & 3 & 2 & 2 \\
$g_3$            & 1 & 1 & 0 & $g_1g_3g_4$    & 2 & 2 & 2 \\
$g_4$            & 1 & 1 & 1 & $g_2g_3g_4$    & 2 & 2 & 2 \\
$g_1g_2$         & 2 & 2 & 2 & $g_1g_2g_3g_4$ & 3 & 3 & 3 \\
$g_1g_3$         & 1 & 2 & 1 & & & & \\
$g_1g_4$         & 2 & 2 & 2 & & & & \\
$g_2g_3$         & 1 & 2 & 1 & & & & \\
$g_2g_4$         & 2 & 2 & 2 & & & & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each column is $0$ at $\emptyset$ and has every marginal in $\set{0,1}$, so all
three are negative dichotomous in cost form.

\emph{The utilitarian optimum.} Agent~1 pays $1$ for every singleton, so
$\cost_1(\bundle{1}) = 0$ only if $\bundle{1} = \emptyset$; agent~3 pays $0$ for
$\set{g_3}$ but $1$ for every superset, so $\cost_3(\bundle{3}) = 0$ only if
$\bundle{3} \subseteq \set{g_3}$. Any allocation of total cost below $2$ would
need at least two agents at cost $0$, forcing $\bundle{1} = \emptyset$ and
$\bundle{3} \subseteq \set{g_3}$ and hence $\bundle{2} \supseteq
\set{g_1,g_2,g_4}$, which costs agent~2 at least $2$. So the minimum total cost
is $2$, attained only by
\[
  \alloc^{\star} \;=\; \big(\emptyset,\ \set{g_1,g_2,g_4},\ \set{g_3}\big),
  \qquad 0 + 2 + 0 = 2 .
\]
Its envy graph has $\edgew{\alloc^\star}(2,1) = 2 - 0 = 2$, so
$\pathw{\alloc^\star}(2) \ge 2$; in fact the minimum subsidy is
$\optsubsidy = (0,2,0)$.

\emph{A zero-subsidy allocation.} Take
$\allocB = (\set{g_1,g_3},\ \set{g_2},\ \set{g_4})$, of total cost
$1 + 1 + 1 = 3$. Its envy graph has weights
\[
  \big(\edgew{\allocB}(i,j)\big)_{i,j} =
  \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\]
every entry of which is at most $0$. Hence there is no positive-weight cycle,
every directed path has weight at most $0$, and $\optsubsidy = (0,0,0)$.
\end{proof}

Two features make this sharper than a bare counterexample. The
utilitarian-optimal allocation is \emph{unique}, so there is no tie to break and
no selection rule inside the optimal set can rescue the conjecture. And the gap
is the largest possible in one step: subsidy $2$ against subsidy $0$, at a cost
of one extra unit of total burden. Restricting attention to utilitarian-optimal
allocations is therefore not a neutral simplification but an actively wrong one.

\begin{remark}[What this rules out]
\label{rem:msw-deadend}
Conjecture~\ref{conj:main}, if true, is \emph{not} witnessed by
welfare-maximising allocations, and the programme of finding a tie-breaking rule
inside the utilitarian-optimal set is unsound as posed --- on this instance the
set is a singleton and the witness lies outside it. The exchange property quoted
at the head of this section remains true and remains usable; what fails is the
assumption that a witness may be sought among cost-minimising allocations at
all. Any search procedure must be free to give up total cost.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Selection rules that fail}
\label{sec:tiebreaks}

Before Proposition~\ref{prop:msw-false} was found, three selection rules inside
the utilitarian-optimal set were tested, and all three fail on instances where
some optimal allocation \emph{does} work. They are recorded because each is a
natural first guess.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Minimising total perceived load} $\Psi(\alloc) := \sum_{i}
    \sum_{j} \cost_j(\bundle{i})$, which prefers bundles that are cheap in
    everybody's opinion rather than only in their holder's.
  \item \textbf{Leximin on the perceived-load vector} $\big(\max_j
    \cost_j(\bundle{i})\big)_{i \in \agents}$, which tries to prevent any single
    bundle from looking heavy to anyone.
  \item \textbf{Local search by single chore transfers} on the objective
    $\big(\max_i \pathw{\alloc}(i), \sum_i \pathw{\alloc}(i)\big)$ restricted to
    the utilitarian-optimal set. This has bad local minima: there are optimal
    allocations with subsidy $2$ from which no single transfer that stays
    optimal improves the objective.
\end{itemize}

The local-search failure is the informative one. Single transfers are too coarse
a move set, because the utilitarian-optimal allocations of a dichotomous
instance are the bases of a structure whose natural moves are exchange
\emph{paths and cycles}, not single steps --- the same phenomenon that makes
Yankee Swap and matroid union the right tools in the matroid-rank
literature~\cite{BEF21}. A move set matched to that structure is the obvious
thing to try next, and remains untried: exchange-path local search over the full
allocation lattice --- not restricted to the utilitarian-optimal set, per
Remark~\ref{rem:msw-deadend} --- with the counterexample hunt re-run under it.
