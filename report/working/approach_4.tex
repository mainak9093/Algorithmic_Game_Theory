\section{Approach 4: Encoding Coverage into the Numbers}
\label{sec:approach4}

\paragraph{The idea.} Corollary~\ref{cor:coverage} reduces
Conjecture~\ref{conj:main} to a single constraint: the replica instance
$\hat{I}$ must admit an allocation in which no agent holds two copies of one
type. Applying~\cite{BKNS22} to $\hat{I}$ is legal but returns an allocation free
to violate that. So change the numbers until a violation becomes unattractive ---
either at the level of the valuations, by charging for duplicates, or at the level
of the algorithm, by inflating weights so the wrong recipient is never selected.

\paragraph{Why it looks promising.} It would let a published algorithm be reused
as a black box, with the only new work being the choice of penalty. And the
penalty has an obvious shape: with $d(S) := \abs{S} - \abs{\tau(S)}$ counting
duplicated copies, charge $u_i = \hat{v}_i + \varepsilon d$.

\paragraph{Where it fails, at the valuation level.} The uniform penalty is a
potential and therefore invisible.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:w3}
Let $u_i = \hat{v}_i + \varepsilon d$ for every $i$, with the same $\varepsilon$
and $d$ for all agents. Then $\edgew{B}^u(i,k) = \edgew{B}^{\hat{v}}(i,k) +
\varepsilon(d(B_k) - d(B_i))$, and hence: \emph{(1)} every directed cycle has the
same weight in both graphs, so a uniform penalty can never \emph{disqualify} an
allocation; \emph{(2)} the agent holding the most duplicates gets weakly
\emph{smaller} subsidy --- the penalty lands on the wrong agents; \emph{(3)} if
$d(B_1) = \dots = d(B_n)$ the two graphs are identical, whatever $\varepsilon$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The identity is immediate. The correction telescopes to $0$ around any cycle,
giving (1), and to endpoint minus start along a path, giving (2). For (3) all
corrections vanish.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Maximally non-coverage, and invisible]
\label{ex:w3}
Take $n = m = 3$ with $\cost_i(S) = \abs{S}$ for every $i$, and let $B_i$ be both
copies of $b_i$. Then $d(B_i) = 1$ for all $i$, so
Theorem~\ref{thm:w3}(3) applies: every arc is $0$ and $\pathw{B}^u = (0,0,0)$.
Yet every chore has two owners --- as far from coverage as possible. Applying
\cite{BKNS22} to this $u$ may return exactly this allocation.
\end{example}

Dropping uniformity does not help, and this is the counterexample worth carrying:

\begin{theorem}[No-go]
\label{thm:w4}
There is a negative dichotomous instance on which \emph{no} faithful reweighting
separates: $n = 3$, $m = 2$, $\cost_1 = \cost_2 = \cost_3 = \abs{\cdot}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:w4prime}
On the same instance separation fails even if faithfulness is dropped entirely.
Among all $990$ dichotomous valuations on the four replica copies, none of the
$990^3$ triples $(u_1,u_2,u_3)$ excludes all twelve non-coverage allocations of
the shape ``one duplicated pair plus two singletons''. Exhaustive, by
\texttt{dupsep.py}: collapsing the $990$ valuations by their behaviour on the
critical family leaves $30$ classes, and $0$ class-triples survive.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Where it fails, at the algorithm level.} Steering the algorithm by
inflating weights fails for a matching reason.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:f2}
Suppose the algorithm of~\cite{BKNS22} reaches its \textsc{FindSink} branch, i.e.\
$(\alloc,\subsidy)$ is not extendable with $g$. Then every $\ell \in
\Mset(\subsidy)$ satisfies $v_\ell(\bundle{\ell} \cup \set{g}) -
v_\ell(\bundle{\ell}) = 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:f3}
Let $s$ be the agent returned by \textsc{FindSink} and $\alloc' =
(\bundle{1},\dots,\bundle{s} \cup \set{g},\dots,\bundle{n})$. Then
$\pathw{\alloc'}(s) = \pathw{\alloc}(s)$.
\end{corollary}

So in the branch that matters the recipient's marginal is already $0$: the
weights the proposal wants to inflate are the ones the algorithm has already
driven to zero, and no inflation can change which agent is selected.

\subsection{Why both fail: holder-blindness}
\label{sec:holderblind}

The two failures have one cause, and naming it is the useful output of this
section.

A duplicate penalty bites only through the arcs. Agent $k$ charging $+1$ for the
duplicates in bundle $B_x$ adds $+1$ to $\edgew{}(k,x)$ --- but the same charge
levied by $x$ on her own bundle adds $+1$ to $u_x(B_x)$, which is $-1$ on every
arc \emph{out of} $x$. The two cancel around every cycle. So a penalty can
disqualify an allocation only if the agent who \emph{holds} the duplicate does
not feel it while somebody else does. But $u$ is fixed before the allocation is
chosen, and it is the allocation that decides who holds the duplicate. Requiring
the holder to be blind for every possible holder forces the penalty to zero.

\begin{remark}[Verdict]
\label{rem:w5}
\textbf{Ruled out:} encoding coverage into the valuations so that~\cite{BKNS22}
can be used as a black box (Theorem~\ref{thm:w4prime}), and steering that
algorithm by inflating weights (Theorem~\ref{thm:f2}). Together these close the
family ``encode coverage into the numbers and reuse~\cite{BKNS22} as a black
box'' from both directions.

\textbf{Not ruled out:} coverage-aware \emph{algorithms}. Coverage is a
constraint on the allocation, and the right place to enforce such a constraint is
the procedure that builds it, not the objective it is scored against --- which is
exactly what \Cref{sec:peelsweep-done} does.

The general shape of the failure is the same as in
\Cref{sec:approach1}: \emph{a rule fixed ex ante against a quantity determined ex
post}. There the owner was fixed before its consequences were visible; here the
penalty must be levied on an agent who cannot be identified in advance.
\end{remark}
