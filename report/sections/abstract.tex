\begin{abstract}
We study the problem of allocating indivisible \emph{chores} among agents in a
fair manner, with subsidies. Envy-free allocations of indivisible items are not
guaranteed to exist, but envy-freeness can be achieved by additionally providing
some subsidy to the agents; in the chores setting this subsidy is naturally read
as compensation paid to the agents who absorb the workload. We study the subsidy
required under \emph{negative dichotomous} valuations, i.e., among agents whose
marginal disutility for any chore is either zero or one:
$v_i(S) - v_i(S \cup \set{g}) \in \set{0,1}$ for every agent $i$, every
$S \subseteq \items$ and every $g \in \items \setminus S$. This is the exact
mirror of the dichotomous goods model of Barman et al.~\cite{BKNS22}, who prove
that there a per-agent subsidy of $0$ or $1$ always suffices.

The mirror is not a relabelling. Subsidies are one-signed --- money is always a
good --- so negating an instance negates the valuations but not the money. We
conjecture that the $\set{0,1}$ guarantee survives the passage to chores, show
that the matching lower bound of $n-1$ does, and prove the conjecture for binary
additive costs, for identical costs, and for two agents. Our main results,
however, are negative, and they concern the two proof strategies a reader would
try first.

First, the item-by-item insertion template of~\cite{BKNS22} --- allocate one item
at a time, never move an allocated item, repair only by permuting whole bundles
--- provably cannot be completed. We exhibit a three-agent instance with a legal
partial state and an unallocated chore for which \emph{every} choice of receiving
agent forces a subsidy of $2$, although the full instance admits a zero-subsidy
allocation. Any correct proof must therefore be free to re-open bundles that have
already been allocated. Second, one cannot recover by selecting a
welfare-maximising allocation instead: we give a three-agent, four-chore instance
whose \emph{unique} utilitarian-optimal allocation requires a subsidy of $2$
while an allocation of strictly higher total cost requires none, so a correct
algorithm must also be free to give up total cost. Along the way we record an
arc-update lemma isolating the whole goods/chore asymmetry in two lines, a
two-tier characterisation of $\set{0,1}$ solutions, and a transform exhibiting
the chore problem as exactly the goods problem of~\cite{BKNS22} together with a
cardinality-balancing requirement --- the precise sense in which the conjecture
is not a corollary of that work.
\end{abstract}
