\section{Unit subsidies are incompatible with Pareto optimality}
\label{app:pareto-sec}

Recall that, in cost form, a complete allocation $\allocB$ \emph{Pareto
dominates} a complete allocation $\alloc$ if
$\cost_i(\allocB_i) \le \cost_i(\bundle i)$ for every $i \in \agents$, with
strict inequality for at least one, and that $\alloc$ is \emph{Pareto optimal}
if no complete allocation dominates it. The quantifier ranges over complete
allocations: for chores, withholding an item weakly benefits every agent, so
allowing incomplete allocations would leave no allocation Pareto optimal.

\begin{example}[Unit subsidies and Pareto optimality are incompatible]
\label{app:pareto}
Consider two agents $\agents = \set{1,2}$ and three chores
$\items = \set{a,b,c}$, with both agents sharing the cost function
\[
  \cost_1(S) = \cost_2(S) = \min\set{\abs S,\ 2},
  \qquad\text{that is}\qquad
  \begin{array}{c|cccc}
    \abs S & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
    \hline
    \cost_i(S) & 0 & 1 & 2 & 2
  \end{array}
\]
Every marginal cost belongs to $\set{0,1}$, so this is a dichotomous cost
function; it is not additive, since $\cost_i(\set{a,b,c}) = 2 \ne 3$.

Because the two agents share one cost function, envy-freeness of
$(\alloc,\subsidy)$ reads $\cost(\bundle1) - \subsidy_1 = \cost(\bundle2) - \subsidy_2$,
so the subsidy difference is pinned to the cost difference and the minimal
subsidy is $\optsubsidy_i = \max\set{0,\ \cost(\bundle i) - \cost(\bundle j)}$.
The eight complete allocations are therefore as follows.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llccccl}
$\bundle1$ & $\bundle2$ & costs & $\optsubsidy$ & $\optsubsidy \le \mathbf 1$ & PO & dominated by \\
\hline
$\set{a,b,c}$ & $\emptyset$ & $(2,0)$ & $(2,0)$ & \textbf{no} & \textbf{yes} & --- \\
$\set{a,b}$ & $\set{c}$ & $(2,1)$ & $(1,0)$ & yes & no & $(\set{a,b,c},\ \emptyset)$ \\
$\set{a,c}$ & $\set{b}$ & $(2,1)$ & $(1,0)$ & yes & no & $(\set{a,b,c},\ \emptyset)$ \\
$\set{b,c}$ & $\set{a}$ & $(2,1)$ & $(1,0)$ & yes & no & $(\set{a,b,c},\ \emptyset)$ \\
$\set{a}$ & $\set{b,c}$ & $(1,2)$ & $(0,1)$ & yes & no & $(\emptyset,\ \set{a,b,c})$ \\
$\set{b}$ & $\set{a,c}$ & $(1,2)$ & $(0,1)$ & yes & no & $(\emptyset,\ \set{a,b,c})$ \\
$\set{c}$ & $\set{a,b}$ & $(1,2)$ & $(0,1)$ & yes & no & $(\emptyset,\ \set{a,b,c})$ \\
$\emptyset$ & $\set{a,b,c}$ & $(0,2)$ & $(0,2)$ & \textbf{no} & \textbf{yes} & --- \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

No allocation is both Pareto optimal and envy-free with a subsidy of at most
one per agent.
\end{example}

The two columns fail for the same reason. The cost function saturates at
$\abs S = 2$, so the third chore is free to an agent who already holds two:
concentrating all three chores on one agent costs $2$ in total, whereas every
split costs $3$, which is why exactly the two concentrated allocations are
Pareto optimal and every split is dominated by one of them. But that same
saturation makes the loaded agent's cost exceed the empty agent's by $2$, so
each concentrated allocation needs a subsidy of $2$.

Consequently the unit-subsidy guarantee of Theorem~\ref{thm:m-main} cannot be
strengthened to deliver a Pareto optimal allocation, and enforcing it costs
efficiency: on this instance the cheapest admissible allocation has total cost
$3$ against $2$ for the cheapest Pareto optimal one.
